Abstract - Automated resume screening typically relies on 
keyword or lexical matching and produces a single static 
score, with no mechanism to revise that judgment when 
later evidence—such as an interview—contradicts it. 
This paper presents an integrated, locally-deployable AI 
recruitment pipeline combining resume–job description 
matching, adaptive AI interviewing, structured interview

robustness testing, and a local-versus-hosted model 
comparison on quality, latency, and resource usage.

INTRODUCTION 
Large-scale recruitment pipelines depend on an initial 
screening stage to reduce a large applicant pool to a 
shortlist suitable for human review. Traditional applicant 
tracking systems (ATS) perform this screening primarily 
through keyword or rule-based matching between resume 
text and job description requirements [REQUIRED 
CITATION]. More recent systems incorporate statistical 
and neural language representations—ranging from 
term-frequency and lexical-overlap methods to 
transformer-based embedding similarity—to capture 
semantic relevance beyond exact keyword overlap 
[REQUIRED CITATION]. In parallel, the emergence of 
large language models (LLMs) has enabled automated 
generation and evaluation of open-ended interview 
responses, extending automation from document 
screening into structured candidate assessment 
[REQUIRED CITATION]. 
Despite this progress, existing systems predominantly 
treat resume screening and interview evaluation as 
independent, sequential filters rather than as a single 
pipeline in which later evidence can revise an earlier 
judgment. A resume-based score is typically computed 
once and used to gate access to subsequent stages, but 
once a candidate proceeds to interview, prior work offers 
little methodological basis for asking whether the 
interview stage actually corrects errors present in the 
initial resume-based ranking—for example, a resume that 
overstates a candidate's competence, or one that 
understates it. Existing evaluations of recruitment AI 
systems also tend to report accuracy or agreement for a 
single component in isolation (screening or interview 
scoring), rather than auditing the consistency of the 
ranking across pipeline stages, and rarely report 
robustness to adversarial resume content such as 
keyword inflation or prompt injection within the same 
evaluation framework [REQUIRED CITATION]. This 
leaves an open question as to whether integrating 
adaptive interviewing with resume screening yields a 
measurably more consistent candidate ranking than 
resume screening alone, or whether it simply adds 
computational and operational cost without a 
demonstrable correction effect.

locally-deployable recruitment pipeline that combines

                                     LITERATURE SURVEY  
Automated recruitment technology has evolved through 
several distinct methodological generations, moving 
progressively from surface-level text matching toward 
context-sensitive, model-driven candidate evaluation. 
Early systems were designed primarily to manage 
applicant volume rather than to assess candidate quality 
with any semantic depth, and this design goal has 
continued to shape the field even as the underlying 
language technology has changed substantially. The 
following review traces this evolution across four related 
but distinct lines of work — rule-based applicant tracking, 
semantic and embedding-based matching, transformer- 
and LLM-based recruitment systems, and the 
explainability, fairness, and security concerns that these 
more capable systems have introduced — before 
identifying where the present work situates itself relative 
to this trajectory. 
The earliest generation of applicant tracking systems 
(ATS) treated resume screening chiefly as a document-
retrieval and scoring problem, ranking candidates by the 
presence or frequency of employer-specified keywords and 
criteria [1]. Tiwari et al. [1] describe an applicant tracking 
and scoring architecture representative of this generation, 
in which resumes are parsed into structured fields and 
compared against job requirements through largely 
deterministic matching logic. This approach offered clear 
advantages in processing speed and auditability, since the 
scoring criteria were explicit and reproducible, but it 
provided no mechanism for recognizing that two 
differently worded skill descriptions might refer to the 
same underlying competency — a limitation that motivated 
the subsequent shift toward semantic representations. 
Subsequent work sought to close this semantic gap by 
representing resumes and job descriptions in a shared 
vector space rather than as literal token sequences. 
Pudasaini et al. [3] combined word-embedding 
representations with a Gale–Shapley stable-matching 
procedure, computing cosine similarity between embedded 
resume and job-description text and then resolving the 
resulting bilateral preferences into a stable assignment 
between candidates and positions. This formulation 
reframed screening as an optimization problem over 
mutual preference rather than a one-directional ranking 
task, which is a meaningfully different objective from the 
ranking-only formulation adopted by most later systems, 
including the present work. In contrast, approaches built on 
named-entity recognition and keyword-augmented 
embeddings pursued interpretability more directly by 
explicitly extracting skill and requirement entities before 
computing similarity, trading some of the flexibility of 
purely distributional representations for scores that could 
be traced back to specific extracted terms. Across this line 
of work, a common limitation is that embedding similarity, 
however computed, is optimized to reflect textual 
relatedness rather than an externally validated notion of 
job-fit, so improvements in similarity metrics do not by 
themselves establish improvements in hiring-relevant 
accuracy. 
Contextual transformer models extended this semantic-
matching paradigm by conditioning a candidate's 
representation on the surrounding resume section and, in 
===== PAGE 3 =====
some formulations, on the paired job description jointly 
rather than encoding each document independently. Li et 
al. [2] demonstrated this shift concretely in a large-scale 
resume-classification study, in which a context-aware 
transformer architecture using section encoding and multi-
head attention decoding was evaluated against a corpus of 
6,492 clinical-research-coordinator resumes annotated 
through triple-blind expert review; their models achieved 
73.3% accuracy on competence-level classification and 
79.2% accuracy on the paired resume–job-description 
matching task, with an inter-annotator Kappa of 61% 
establishing that even expert human judgment does not 
converge perfectly on adjacent competence levels. This 
result is significant for the present work in two respects: it 
shows that contextual encoding measurably outperforms 
non-contextual similarity on a rigorously annotated 
dataset, and it establishes that human-agreement ceilings, 
not only model capacity, bound how much matching 
accuracy can improve — a consideration directly relevant 
to the human-agreement experiments proposed in Section 
IV. 
The most recent generation of systems replaces fixed 
embedding comparison with generative, agentic LLM-
based evaluation. Gan et al. [5] introduced an LLM-agent 
framework that summarizes and grades resumes at scale, 
reporting screening roughly eleven times faster than 
manual review and an F1 score of 87.7% on resume-
sentence classification after fine-tuning, with LLM agents 
additionally participating in downstream offer-stage 
decision-making. Extending this direction toward 
interpretability, subsequent multi-agent designs 
decompose the screening task across specialized extractor, 
evaluator, and summarizer agents, in some cases 
incorporating retrieval-augmented generation to ground 
scores in external role-specific criteria rather than relying 
solely on parametric knowledge [6]. Compared with the 
single-pass embedding-similarity methods discussed 
above, these LLM-agent architectures produce natural-
language justifications alongside numeric scores, which is 
a qualitatively different output than a similarity scalar; 
however, this capability introduces a corresponding 
evaluation burden, since a generated justification's fluency 
does not guarantee that it accurately reflects the reasoning 
that produced the associated score. 
The growing capability and deployment scale of these 
systems has drawn increased scrutiny of their fairness and 
robustness properties. Fabris et al. [7] provide a 
multidisciplinary survey of fairness and bias across the 
algorithmic hiring pipeline, cataloguing domain-specific 
bias sources, mitigation techniques, and the legal 
frameworks — including EU and US regulatory 
instruments — that govern their deployment, while 
explicitly noting that whether algorithmic hiring is less 
biased than the human-driven alternatives it replaces 
remains, in their assessment, an open and evidence-
dependent question rather than a settled conclusion. 
Separately, the generative and instruction-following nature 
of LLM-based screening has introduced a security 
dimension absent from earlier keyword- and embedding-
based systems: Baxi et al. [8] show experimentally that 
self-promotional prompt-injection text embedded in a 
résumé can reliably improve an applicant's LLM-assigned 
ranking when few candidates employ the tactic and résumé 
quality is otherwise homogeneous, but that this advantage 
collapses as injection becomes widespread, and can in 
heterogeneous-quality settings occasionally invert the 
ranking of otherwise weaker and stronger candidates — a 
finding with direct fairness implications once manipulation 
is unevenly distributed across an applicant pool. 
Across these four lines of work, resume screening and 
interview-stage evaluation continue to be treated 
predominantly as independent problems: fairness and 
robustness studies examine screening or generation in 
isolation, and LLM-agent frameworks that incorporate 
multiple pipeline stages report efficiency and classification 
accuracy rather than auditing whether a later stage corrects 
errors introduced by an earlier one. None of the reviewed 
work formulates a controlled test of whether an interview 
stage measurably revises a resume-only ranking that later 
evidence contradicts, and the robustness finding in [8] is 
reported for screening in isolation rather than for a pipeline 
in which a subsequent interview stage might counteract an 
injection-inflated resume score. The present work 
addresses this gap directly by treating resume screening 
and adaptive interviewing as stages of a single auditable 
ranking process and by defining rank-shift and recovery-
rate metrics that test, under controlled over-seller and 
hidden-gem conditions, whether the interview stage 
corrects — rather than merely supplements — the resume-
based screening judgment reviewed in this section.

A. System Overview 
Fig. 1 illustrates the architecture of the proposed 
recruitment pipeline, which was structured as a sequence 
of loosely coupled services rather than a monolithic model.

system performed (i) resume parsing and structured field

independently testable service so that individual

CONCLUSION 
In essence, the described AI-powered recruitment solution 
combines the modern large language model features with a 
structured system design for producing highly accurate and 
context-dependent evaluations of applicants. Thus, the 
designed architecture manages to strike a good balance 
between intelligence and efficiency by making it possible 
to process resumes, analyze interview responses, and 
monitor candidate behavior in a single flow. The current 
approach stands out from the traditional solutions, which 
rely on keyword searching or manual processing of 
applications. Instead, by using LLM with context 
understanding capability, the proposed system provides 
deeper insights into candidate qualifications and 
experience and allows evaluating applicants objectively. 
To be specific, the backbone of the suggested system is the 
LLM, which has been installed locally on an Ollama server 
using the LLaMA 3.1 model. Resumes are processed using 
the PyMuPDF (fitz) library to extract necessary pieces of 
text, such as skills, experience, and educational 
background. The extracted data will be analyzed using the 
AI model without any dependence on key terms or 
individual factors. Indeed, just like a human recruiter 
would do when looking at the applicant's profile, the model 
would understand the interrelationships between the 
mentioned factors. 
FUTURE SCOPE  
The described system proves to be a highly promising 
solution in the field of AI recruitment but there is still a 
number of improvements that could potentially be made. 
First of all, an opportunity to implement learning within 
this framework should be considered since the model can 
improve with time when receiving feedback from hiring 
officers and recruiters. It may prove to be beneficial to 
employ either reinforcement learning or some other 
learning algorithms in this case. Secondly, natural 
language processing capabilities of this solution can be 
further enhanced with more sophisticated methods used by 
transformers and state-of-the-art models for fine-tuning 
and prompt engineering. The same goes for creating new 
datasets and implementing NER and semantic matching in 
order to increase parsing accuracy. Thirdly, there are a 
couple of improvements that can be implemented to further 
develop the modules related to interviews and proctoring. 
They include adding support for more complicated tasks 
like speech recognition, sentiment analysis, and facial 
expressions recognition in order to evaluate not only the 
contents but also the way candidates communicate with the 
interviewer. As for the latter, more advanced anomaly 
detection techniques need to be employed. From the 
standpoint of system design, it may prove to be useful to 
replace JSON-based file structure and integrate some kind 
of database management into the design (e.g., PostgreSQL 
or MongoDB). This will make the solution scalable enough 
to handle large amounts of recruitment data. Moreover, it 
may be interesting to try implementing deployment to a 
cloud environment along with containers (Docker). 
Finally, it seems that it might prove useful to further evolve 
the created solution into the recruitment assistant that will 
automate various tasks from sending job descriptions to 
generating questions and communicating with candidates, 
thus fully automating the hiring process.
